# Chapter 1 — Dynamical Systems and Kepler Problems

## HW1.1 — Rediscovering Kepler's Orbit of Mars

This homework studies how orbital information can be reconstructed from observational data.

The project uses a simplified numerical version of historical planetary observations and develops a computational workflow for recovering the geometry of Mars's orbit. The work emphasizes the relationship between physical modeling, data generation, geometric reasoning, and numerical visualization.

### Main ideas

- synthetic observational data;
- Keplerian orbital geometry;
- numerical reconstruction;
- visualization of planetary motion;
- checking a mathematical model against generated data.

---

## HW1.2 — The Flat-Earth Nonholonomic Kepler Problem

This homework studies a modified Kepler problem in which a disk is constrained to move only in the direction of its heading.

The system is nonholonomic because the velocity is constrained by a no-slip condition. The reduced equations are derived for position, forward speed, heading angle, and angular velocity, and the resulting system is simulated for several initial conditions.

### Main ideas

- nonholonomic constraints;
- reduced equations of motion;
- numerical ODE simulation;
- energy conservation;
- orbital angular momentum;
- heading angular momentum;
- comparison with the ordinary Kepler problem.

An important observation is that an ideal nonholonomic constraint may preserve energy while ordinary orbital angular momentum is not generally conserved.

# Chapter 2 — Foundations of Probability

## HW2.1 — Casino Probability, Sigma-Algebras, and Bad Decisions

This homework uses roulette and a simplified blackjack model as probability laboratories.

The objective is not to develop a gambling strategy, but to practice constructing probability spaces, defining events and random variables, computing expectations and variances, and solving a bankroll-allocation optimization problem.

Monte Carlo simulations are used to verify the theoretical results.

### Main ideas

- sample spaces and sigma-algebras;
- probability events;
- payoff random variables;
- expectation and variance;
- European roulette;
- simplified blackjack;
- bankroll allocation;
- Monte Carlo verification.

For the models studied, both roulette and blackjack have negative expected payoff, so the expected-value maximizing decision is to bet nothing.

---

## HW2.2 — Throwing Good Money After Bad

This homework studies the **gambler's fallacy** and loss-chasing strategies.

The first part models independent roulette spins and shows that a previous losing streak does not change the probability of winning the next spin. A deliberately incorrect LLM argument is then analyzed to identify how plausible mathematical language can be used to justify a false conclusion.

The final numerical experiment studies a Martingale-style betting strategy with a finite bankroll and a table limit.

### Main ideas

- independent random variables;
- conditional probability;
- gambler's fallacy;
- European versus American roulette;
- law of large numbers;
- misleading probabilistic reasoning;
- Martingale betting;
- finite bankroll and table limits;
- rare large losses versus frequent small gains.

A major lesson is that a strategy can win frequently while still having negative expected value because rare losses can be much larger than the common gains.

# Chapter 3 — Optimal Transport

## HW3.1 — Bakeries, Cafés, and the Difference Between Monge and Kantorovich

This homework introduces discrete optimal transport through a synthetic bakery–café transportation problem.

Bakeries have fixed supplies, cafés have fixed demands, and transportation costs are determined from distances between locations.

The Monge and Kantorovich formulations are then compared computationally.

### Main ideas

- discrete probability mass transport;
- Euclidean transportation costs;
- Monge transport maps;
- Kantorovich transport plans;
- linear programming;
- feasibility of transport problems;
- mass splitting;
- comparison of the cases `N > M` and `N < M`.

The experiments demonstrate an important distinction:

- a **Monge map** requires each source to send all of its mass to a single destination;
- a **Kantorovich plan** allows a source to split its mass among several destinations.

When there are fewer bakeries than cafés and every café has positive demand, a Monge map may be impossible, while the Kantorovich problem can remain feasible.

## HW3.2 — Benamou–Brenier in One Dimension

This homework studies the dynamic interpretation of optimal transport.

In one dimension, the optimal transport map is obtained from CDFs and quantile functions:

$$
T(x)=F_1^{-1}(F_0(x)).
$$

The Wasserstein geodesic is generated by displacement interpolation:

$$
X_t(x)=(1-t)x+tT(x).
$$

The experiments first transport a compact half-circle density to a uniform density and then transport it to a Gaussian target.

### Main ideas

- CDFs and quantile functions;
- monotone optimal transport;
- displacement interpolation;
- Wasserstein geodesics;
- particle velocities;
- Benamou–Brenier action;
- compact support versus Gaussian tails;
- numerical treatment of unbounded distributions.

The numerical computation verifies that the dynamic Benamou–Brenier action agrees with the squared Wasserstein distance up to numerical error.

The Gaussian experiment also highlights an important numerical issue: a Gaussian has support on all of `R`, so a finite computational grid must be treated as a truncation rather than as the full distribution.

# Chapter 4 — Wasserstein Gradient Flows

## HW4.1 — Barenblatt Solutions and the Porous Medium Equation

This homework studies the Barenblatt self-similar solutions of the porous medium equation

$$
\partial_t \rho = \Delta(\rho^m), \qquad m>1.
$$

The similarity exponents are derived using mass conservation and scaling, and the coefficient in the Barenblatt profile is verified by direct substitution into the PDE.

The resulting solution is then simulated and analyzed.

### Main ideas

- self-similar solutions;
- scaling analysis;
- mass conservation;
- Barenblatt profiles;
- compact support;
- free boundaries;
- finite-speed propagation;
- weak solutions;
- Wasserstein gradient flows;
- Otto calculus.

The numerical experiments verify the coefficients and demonstrate that the solution spreads while remaining compactly supported at every finite positive time.

The homework also studies regularity of the Barenblatt profile. It is smooth where the density is positive but generally loses smoothness at the moving free boundary. This is a characteristic feature of degenerate nonlinear diffusion rather than a defect of the solution.

## HW4.2 — An Explicit JKO Step for the Heat Equation

This homework studies an explicit Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto (JKO) step for the heat equation by restricting the problem to centered Gaussian densities.

For

$$
\rho=\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),
$$

the entropy functional becomes, up to an additive constant,

$$
\mathcal E(\rho)=-\log\sigma,
$$

and the squared Wasserstein distance between centered Gaussians is

$$
W_2^2
\left(
\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),
\mathcal N(0,\sigma_k^2)
\right)
=
(\sigma-\sigma_k)^2.
$$

This reduces the JKO minimization to a one-variable optimization problem with the explicit update

$$
\sigma_{k+1}
=
\frac{\sigma_k+\sqrt{\sigma_k^2+4\tau}}{2}.
$$

### Main ideas

- entropy of a Gaussian;
- Wasserstein distance between Gaussians;
- JKO minimization;
- convex optimization;
- exact Gaussian heat flow;
- variance growth;
- time discretization;
- convergence as `tau -> 0`.

The discrete JKO variance is compared with the exact heat-equation variance

$$
\sigma^2(t)=\sigma_0^2+2t.
$$

The numerical experiments show that the JKO approximation converges toward the exact solution as the time step decreases, with approximately first-order convergence.

# Computational Workflow

The general workflow used throughout the repository is:

1. derive the relevant mathematical model;
2. use an LLM when requested by the assignment to suggest an explanation, derivation, or implementation;
3. independently check the mathematical claims;
4. implement a numerical experiment in Python;
5. compare theoretical and numerical results;
6. save generated numerical data when applicable;
7. save figures from the experiment;
8. incorporate the derivation, verification, plots, and LLM critique into the final homework report.

This structure makes the numerical experiments reproducible and separates the computational work from the written reports.

---

# LLM Verification Philosophy

A major theme of these assignments is the critical use of large language models.

The LLM is treated as a mathematical assistant rather than as a source of ground truth. Its responses are checked through methods such as:

- direct algebraic derivation;
- substitution into governing equations;
- conditional-probability calculations;
- numerical residuals;
- Monte Carlo simulation;
- conservation-law checks;
- independent numerical optimization;
- limiting arguments;
- comparison with known exact solutions.

Several assignments intentionally ask the LLM to produce or analyze misleading reasoning. These exercises are used to identify common failure modes, including incorrect conditional probability, confusion between related mathematical quantities, unjustified assumptions, and mathematically plausible but false arguments.
