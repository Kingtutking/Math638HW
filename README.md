# Chapter 1 — Dynamical Systems and Kepler Problems

## HW1.1 — Rediscovering Kepler's Orbit of Mars

This homework studies how orbital information can be reconstructed from observational data.

The project uses a simplified numerical version of historical planetary observations and develops a computational workflow for recovering the geometry of Mars's orbit. The work emphasizes the relationship between physical modeling, data generation, geometric reasoning, and numerical visualization.

### Main ideas

- synthetic observational data;
- Keplerian orbital geometry;
- numerical reconstruction;
- visualization of planetary motion;
- checking a mathematical model against generated data.

## HW1.2 — The Flat-Earth Nonholonomic Kepler Problem

This homework studies a modified Kepler problem in which a disk is constrained to move only in the direction of its heading.

The system is nonholonomic because the velocity is constrained by a no-slip condition. The reduced equations are derived for position, forward speed, heading angle, and angular velocity, and the resulting system is simulated for several initial conditions.

### Main ideas

- nonholonomic constraints;
- reduced equations of motion;
- numerical ODE simulation;
- energy conservation;
- orbital angular momentum;
- heading angular momentum;
- comparison with the ordinary Kepler problem.

An important observation is that an ideal nonholonomic constraint may preserve energy while ordinary orbital angular momentum is not generally conserved.

# Chapter 2 — Foundations of Probability

## HW2.1 — Casino Probability, Sigma-Algebras, and Bad Decisions

This homework uses roulette and a simplified blackjack model as probability laboratories.

The objective is not to develop a gambling strategy, but to practice constructing probability spaces, defining events and random variables, computing expectations and variances, and solving a bankroll-allocation optimization problem.

Monte Carlo simulations are used to verify the theoretical results.

### Main ideas

- sample spaces and sigma-algebras;
- probability events;
- payoff random variables;
- expectation and variance;
- European roulette;
- simplified blackjack;
- bankroll allocation;
- Monte Carlo verification.

For the models studied, both roulette and blackjack have negative expected payoff, so the expected-value maximizing decision is to bet nothing.

## HW2.2 — Throwing Good Money After Bad

This homework studies the **gambler's fallacy** and loss-chasing strategies.

The first part models independent roulette spins and shows that a previous losing streak does not change the probability of winning the next spin. A deliberately incorrect LLM argument is then analyzed to identify how plausible mathematical language can be used to justify a false conclusion.

The final numerical experiment studies a Martingale-style betting strategy with a finite bankroll and a table limit.

### Main ideas

- independent random variables;
- conditional probability;
- gambler's fallacy;
- European versus American roulette;
- law of large numbers;
- misleading probabilistic reasoning;
- Martingale betting;
- finite bankroll and table limits;
- rare large losses versus frequent small gains.

A major lesson is that a strategy can win frequently while still having negative expected value because rare losses can be much larger than the common gains.

# Chapter 3 — Optimal Transport

## HW3.1 — Bakeries, Cafés, and the Difference Between Monge and Kantorovich

This homework introduces discrete optimal transport through a synthetic bakery–café transportation problem.

Bakeries have fixed supplies, cafés have fixed demands, and transportation costs are determined from distances between locations.

The Monge and Kantorovich formulations are then compared computationally.

### Main ideas

- discrete probability mass transport;
- Euclidean transportation costs;
- Monge transport maps;
- Kantorovich transport plans;
- linear programming;
- feasibility of transport problems;
- mass splitting;
- comparison of the cases `N > M` and `N < M`.

The experiments demonstrate an important distinction:

- a **Monge map** requires each source to send all of its mass to a single destination;
- a **Kantorovich plan** allows a source to split its mass among several destinations.

When there are fewer bakeries than cafés and every café has positive demand, a Monge map may be impossible while the Kantorovich problem can remain feasible.

---

## HW3.2 — Benamou–Brenier in One Dimension

This homework studies the dynamic interpretation of optimal transport.

In one dimension, the optimal transport map is obtained from CDFs and quantile functions:

```math
T(x)=F_1^{-1}(F_0(x)).
```

The Wasserstein geodesic is generated by displacement interpolation:

```math
X_t(x)=(1-t)x+tT(x).
```

The experiments first transport a compact half-circle density to a uniform density and then transport it to a Gaussian target.

### Main ideas

- CDFs and quantile functions;
- monotone optimal transport;
- displacement interpolation;
- Wasserstein geodesics;
- particle velocities;
- Benamou–Brenier action;
- compact support versus Gaussian tails;
- numerical treatment of unbounded distributions.

The numerical computation verifies that the dynamic Benamou–Brenier action agrees with the squared Wasserstein distance up to numerical error.

The Gaussian experiment also highlights an important numerical issue: a Gaussian has support on all of `R`, so a finite computational grid must be treated as a truncation rather than as the full distribution.

# Chapter 4 — Wasserstein Gradient Flows

## HW4.1 — Barenblatt Solutions and the Porous Medium Equation

This homework studies the Barenblatt self-similar solutions of the porous medium equation

```math
\partial_t \rho = \Delta(\rho^m), \qquad m>1.
```

The similarity exponents are derived using mass conservation and scaling, and the coefficient in the Barenblatt profile is verified by direct substitution into the PDE.

The resulting solution is then simulated and analyzed.

### Main ideas

- self-similar solutions;
- scaling analysis;
- mass conservation;
- Barenblatt profiles;
- compact support;
- free boundaries;
- finite-speed propagation;
- weak solutions;
- Wasserstein gradient flows;
- Otto calculus.

The numerical experiments verify the coefficients and demonstrate that the solution spreads while remaining compactly supported at every finite positive time.

The homework also studies regularity of the Barenblatt profile. It is smooth where the density is positive but generally loses smoothness at the moving free boundary.

---

## HW4.2 — An Explicit JKO Step for the Heat Equation

This homework studies an explicit Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto step for the heat equation by restricting the problem to centered Gaussian densities.

For

```math
\rho=\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),
```

the entropy functional becomes, up to an additive constant,

```math
\mathcal E(\rho)=-\log\sigma,
```

and the squared Wasserstein distance between centered Gaussians is

```math
W_2^2
\left(
\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),
\mathcal N(0,\sigma_k^2)
\right)
=
(\sigma-\sigma_k)^2.
```

This reduces the JKO minimization to a one-variable optimization problem with the explicit update

```math
\sigma_{k+1}
=
\frac{\sigma_k+\sqrt{\sigma_k^2+4\tau}}{2}.
```

### Main ideas

- entropy of a Gaussian;
- Wasserstein distance between Gaussians;
- JKO minimization;
- convex optimization;
- exact Gaussian heat flow;
- variance growth;
- time discretization;
- convergence as `tau -> 0`.

The discrete JKO variance is compared with the exact heat-equation variance

```math
\sigma^2(t)=\sigma_0^2+2t.
```

The numerical experiments show that the JKO approximation converges toward the exact solution as the time step decreases, with approximately first-order convergence.

# Chapter 5 — Tokenization, Decipherment, and Next-Token Prediction

## HW5.1 — Syllable-Level Neural Decipherment with Partial Anchors and an Independent English Reference Corpus

This homework constructs an artificial decipherment problem from the complete public-domain text of Marcus Aurelius' *Meditations*.

The cleaned source corpus contains all 12 books of *Meditations*. A written-syllable tokenizer produces **77,260 syllable occurrences** and **3,052 distinct source syllable types**. A seeded random bijection maps those source syllables to neutral artificial glyph labels.

Benjamin Jowett's translation of Plato's *Republic* is used as an independent English reference corpus. The experiment compares an anchor-only system with a two-stage Republic-informed pipeline:

1. infer a candidate substitution map from English statistics and revealed anchors;
2. train an embedding-plus-MLP decoder on the resulting pseudo-labels.

### Main ideas

- text cleaning and corpus verification;
- written-syllable tokenization;
- exact token reconstruction checks;
- artificial substitution ciphers;
- partial anchor information;
- independent reference-corpus statistics;
- pseudo-label generation;
- neural decoding;
- train/validation/test information separation;
- inverse-map recovery;
- LLM criticism and calibration.

At 20% anchors, overall test accuracy increases from about **14.36%** for the anchor-only system to about **26.40%** for the Republic-informed pipeline. However, unanchored-token accuracy remains only about **9.37%**.

Increasing the number of anchors has the strongest effect. At 40% anchors, Republic-informed unanchored accuracy reaches about **15.72%**.

The experiment therefore demonstrates partial benefit from independent English statistics and pseudo-label supervision, but it does **not** demonstrate reliable recovery of the complete hidden substitution map.

The supplementary appendix contains book-by-book corpus checks, frequency tables, and complete evaluation tables.

## HW5.2 — Next-Token Prediction for the Lorenz Dynamical System

This homework converts continuous Lorenz trajectories into discrete token sequences and studies next-token prediction.

The classical Lorenz system is

```math
\dot{x}=\sigma(y-x),
```

```math
\dot{y}=x(\rho-z)-y,
```

```math
\dot{z}=xy-\beta z,
```

with

```math
\sigma=10,
\qquad
\rho=28,
\qquad
\beta=\frac{8}{3}.
```

Sixty independent Lorenz trajectories are generated: 40 training books, 10 validation books, and 10 test books.

The continuous phase space is discretized into a baseline grid of

```math
12\times12\times12
```

boxes, giving 1,728 possible tokens.

Empirical Markov next-token models with context lengths

```math
m=1,2,3
```

are compared.

Validation selects

```math
m=3.
```

### Main ideas

- Lorenz dynamics;
- phase-space discretization;
- token vocabularies;
- trajectory books;
- first- and higher-order Markov models;
- deterministic backoff;
- next-token accuracy;
- autonomous token generation;
- Lorenz lobe switching;
- discretization sensitivity.

The selected order-3 model reaches test next-token accuracy of about **0.759**.

Autonomously generated trajectories also reproduce selected coarse lobe-switching statistics reasonably well. However, the final interpretation is deliberately limited: matching token-level statistics does not establish equality of invariant measures, Lyapunov exponents, entropy, or exact continuous-time Lorenz dynamics.

# Chapter 6 — Attention and Contextual Sequence Models

## HW6.1 — Attention for Tokenized Phase-Space Dynamics

This homework continues the Lorenz tokenization experiment from HW5.2 and replaces fixed-order Markov prediction with a learned causal-attention model.

The main comparison is between:

- the validation-selected order-3 Markov model;
- a small causal self-attention next-token predictor.

All final comparisons use the same held-out target positions.

The selected attention model uses context length

```math
L=32
```

and approximately 188,000 trainable parameters.

### Main ideas

- token embeddings;
- positional embeddings;
- causal self-attention;
- next-token cross-entropy training;
- common-support evaluation;
- attention-weight interpretation;
- temporal-lag analysis;
- autonomous generation;
- comparison between local prediction and long-run dynamics.

On the common test set:

- Markov order 3 accuracy is about **0.7587**;
- attention accuracy is about **0.8392**.

Thus attention improves one-step token prediction by about **8.05 percentage points**.

However, better one-step prediction does not imply better autonomous dynamics. In the equal-prefix generation experiment:

- true held-out trajectories switch Lorenz lobes about **10.96** times per 1000 steps;
- Markov generations switch about **10.76** times;
- attention generations switch about **25.22** times.

The strongest conclusion is therefore:

> Causal attention learns useful nonlocal predictive correlations in tokenized Lorenz trajectories, but higher next-token accuracy does not by itself recover the long-run Lorenz dynamics.

## HW6.2 — Attention, Glyphs, and Contextual Decipherment

This homework returns to the artificial decipherment problem from HW5.1 and asks whether self-attention improves contextual glyph decoding.

The corpus, tokenization, glyph vocabulary, hidden bijection, data split, anchor sets, and permitted supervision are kept fixed.

The modeling change is

```text
fixed-context non-attention decoder
        ->
centered self-attention decoder
```

The selected attention architecture uses:

- embedding dimension 96;
- context radius 4;
- window length 9;
- two attention heads;
- one self-attention layer;
- 664,684 trainable parameters.

### Main ideas

- centered self-attention;
- contextual decipherment;
- matched MLP baselines;
- frozen information regimes;
- bijective versus ambiguous glyphs;
- attention interventions;
- identifiability limits;
- context sensitivity versus overall accuracy.

In the original one-to-one cipher, attention and the matched MLP perform almost identically. At 20% anchors, whole-text test accuracy is about **0.2680** for attention and **0.2673** for the MLP, while unanchored accuracy is the same for both.

A controlled many-to-one glyph experiment creates genuine symbol ambiguity. Attention does not consistently dominate the MLP in total accuracy, but intervention experiments show that the attention model has learned real contextual sensitivity.

For ambiguous centers:

- replacing the highest-attended non-center symbol changes true-label probability by about **0.1925** on average;
- replacing the lowest-attended control changes it by only about **0.0020**;
- the argmax prediction changes in about **37.0%** of top-attention interventions but less than **1%** of low-attention controls.

The main conclusion is that attention can learn meaningful contextual dependence without overcoming the underlying identifiability barrier.

## HW7.2 — From Marcus Aurelius to Homer: Conditioned Text Generation

This homework studies conditioned statistical text generation using:

- Marcus Aurelius' *Meditations* as the source of themes;
- Samuel Butler's prose translation of Homer's *Odyssey* as the target style corpus.

Five themes are fixed before generation:

- discipline;
- mortality;
- anger;
- duty;
- self-command.

The target model uses interpolated word-level unigram, bigram, and trigram statistics.

For each theme, the experiment generates:

- 500 unconditioned samples;
- 500 conditioned samples.

This gives 5,000 statistical generations in total.

The conditioning mechanism applies a fixed multiplicative reward with

```math
\lambda=1.5
```

to predeclared theme words.

### Main ideas

- conditional distributions;
- statistical language models;
- unigram, bigram, and trigram interpolation;
- reproducible conditioning rules;
- fixed seeds;
- rewarded versus unboosted thematic probes;
- bootstrap comparisons;
- Jensen-Shannon distance;
- failure analysis;
- direct LLM comparison.

The conditioned generator reliably increases the frequency of the words it is explicitly rewarded to use.

However, the unboosted thematic probes do not show a reliable increase, and the broad unigram Jensen-Shannon distance is comparable to independent finite-sample baseline variation.

The statistical generator also has substantial fluency limitations, including a high rate of 30-word truncation.

The main conclusion is therefore intentionally narrow:

> The conditioning mechanism changes the distribution of explicitly rewarded words, but the experiment does not establish preservation of the full Marcus proposition, negation, or ethical stance.

# Chapter 8 — Diffusion Models and Stochastic Feedback

## HW8.1 — A Two-Mode Diffusion Generator

This homework builds a complete one-dimensional diffusion-model example around a symmetric two-mode target distribution.

The data modes are centered at

```math
-2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
2,
```

with component standard deviation

```math
\sigma_0=0.25.
```

A forward process merges the two modes into a standard Gaussian. The exact score is derived analytically, reverse probability flow is constructed, and both exact and learned score fields are tested.

### Main ideas

- forward diffusion schedules;
- Gaussian mixtures;
- exact score functions;
- score-field geometry;
- probability-flow ODEs;
- reverse-time sign conventions;
- mode merging;
- exact reverse generation;
- fitted neural score models;
- latent-label information loss;
- LLM derivation audits.

The two visible density peaks merge at approximately

```math
t=0.702823.
```

The exact-score reverse generator accurately reconstructs the target distribution.

The fitted neural score is useful but measurably worse than the exact score and is explicitly reported as a finite-training-budget approximation rather than a fully converged model.

At the final Gaussian endpoint, the probability density remains smooth even though the original two-component representation becomes unidentifiable and the latent component-label information disappears.

## HW8.2 — The Dictator's Dilemma as a Stochastic Feedback Problem

This homework studies a two-state stochastic feedback system

```math
dX_t
=
a(-X_t+L_t)dt
+
\sigma dW_t
```

and

```math
dL_t
=
-\kappa
\frac{|X_t|}
{1+\eta|L_t|}
dt.
```

The controller acts on the perceived deviation

```math
Y_t=X_t-L_t
```

rather than directly on the hidden true state $X_t$.

The baseline parameters are

```math
a=0.5,
\qquad
\kappa=0.05,
\qquad
\sigma=0.2,
\qquad
\eta=1.
```

### Main ideas

- Euler-Maruyama simulation;
- stochastic feedback;
- first-passage failure times;
- censoring and restricted mean survival;
- Fokker-Planck equations;
- weak-generator checks;
- hidden-state reconstruction;
- likelihood inference for unknown initial distortion;
- observation bias;
- generative-AI interpretation;
- LLM long-time prediction audits.

For failure defined by

```math
|X_t|>2,
```

the main simulation estimates a failure probability of about **0.5808** by time 100 and a restricted mean survival time of about **95.31**.

With exact observations, known parameters, and known initial distortion $L_0$, the synthetic hidden path can be reconstructed to floating-point accuracy.

When $L_0$ is unknown, the same problem becomes statistical and the likelihood can remain broad.

The final long-time experiment also shows a striking separation between hidden and perceived behavior. The LLM predicts heuristically that

```math
U_t=-L_t
```

should grow at approximately

```math
\frac{\kappa}{\eta}=0.05
```

per unit time while $Y_t$ behaves approximately like an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with mean 0.1 and variance 0.04.

A fresh holdout simulation gives late-time values close to these predictions while $X_t$ and $L_t$ continue drifting to large negative values.

The finite-horizon simulations support the heuristic, but the report does not present them as a proof of an asymptotic theorem.

# LLM Verification Philosophy

A major theme of the course is the **critical use of large language models**.

The LLM is treated as a mathematical assistant rather than as a source of ground truth.

Its responses are checked through methods such as:

- direct algebraic derivation;
- substitution into governing equations;
- conditional-probability calculations;
- numerical residuals;
- Monte Carlo simulation;
- conservation-law checks;
- weak-generator checks;
- independent numerical optimization;
- held-out test evaluation;
- common-support model comparison;
- seed-controlled experiments;
- bootstrap uncertainty intervals;
- limiting arguments;
- comparison with known exact solutions;
- fresh holdout simulations performed after an LLM prediction is fixed.

Several assignments intentionally expose failure modes that can occur even when an answer sounds mathematically plausible.

Examples include:

- incorrect conditional-probability reasoning;
- confusion between one-step predictive accuracy and autonomous generative quality;
- confusing attention sensitivity with identifiability;
- overinterpreting directly rewarded conditioning words as semantic preservation;
- reverse-time sign errors in diffusion models;
- treating finite-horizon numerical evidence as an asymptotic proof;
- overstating confidence in weakly identified hidden variables.

The general principle is:

> Generated mathematical reasoning should be tested independently rather than accepted because it is fluent or plausible.

1. formulate the mathematical problem precisely;
2. implement the experiment reproducibly;
3. separate fitting information from evaluation information;

5. report both successes and limitations;
